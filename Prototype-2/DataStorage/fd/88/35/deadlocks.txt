A deadlock is a situation where processes are permanently blocked.
It happens when processes hold resources and wait for others.
Mutual exclusion means resources can only be held by one process.
Hold and wait means a process holds resources while requesting more.
No preemption means resources cannot be forcibly taken from processes.
Circular wait means a chain of processes exists waiting circularly.
Resource Allocation Graphs (RAG) visualize resource allocation states.
Cycles in RAG indicate potential deadlocks if resources are single-unit.
Deadlock prevention invalidates one of the four deadlock conditions.
Preventing mutual exclusion is difficult for inherently non-shareable devices.
Preventing hold and wait requires requesting all resources at startup.
Preventing no preemption means releasing held resources if blocked.
Preventing circular wait imposes a strict global ordering on resources.
Deadlock avoidance requires dynamic knowledge of future resource requests.
Safe state means the system can allocate resources without deadlock.
Unsafe state does not guarantee deadlock, but deadlock is possible.
Banker's Algorithm determines if resource requests keep system safe.
It uses matrices representing allocation, max demand, and availability.
Deadlock detection runs periodically to identify active deadlocks.
Once detected, recovery mechanisms must resolve the deadlock state.
Process termination kills deadlocked processes to break the cycle.
Resource preemption rolls back processes to reclaim needed resources.
Ostrich algorithm ignores deadlocks, assuming they occur very rarely.
Many general-purpose operating systems use the ostrich approach.
Robust database engines implement custom active deadlock detection.
