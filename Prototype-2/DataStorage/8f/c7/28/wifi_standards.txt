Wi-Fi standards define wireless network speeds and frequencies.
IEEE 802.11 is the working group maintaining Wi-Fi specifications.
802.11b (1999) operated in 2.4 GHz, reaching 11 Mbps.
802.11a (1999) operated in 5 GHz, reaching 54 Mbps.
802.11g (2003) brought 54 Mbps speeds to 2.4 GHz frequency.
802.11n (Wi-Fi 4) introduced MIMO, reaching 600 Mbps speeds.
Wi-Fi 4 operated in both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands.
802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5) used wider channels in 5 GHz band.
Wi-Fi 5 reached gigabit speeds using multi-user MIMO (MU-MIMO).
802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6) improves efficiency in congested environments.
Wi-Fi 6 uses OFDMA to split channels for multiple clients.
Wi-Fi 6E extends Wi-Fi 6 features to the 6 GHz band.
The 6 GHz band offers wide channels without legacy interference.
802.11be (Wi-Fi 7) is designed for extremely high throughput.
Wi-Fi 7 supports 320 MHz channels and Multi-Link Operation (MLO).
MLO allows devices to send and receive data across bands.
Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM) defines data bits per symbol.
Wi-Fi 6 uses 1024-QAM; Wi-Fi 7 uses 4096-QAM for density.
Channel bonding combines adjacent channels to double bandwidth throughput.
Guard interval prevents inter-symbol interference caused by reflections.
Wi-Fi Alliance certifies device compatibility and security standards.
Backward compatibility ensures old devices connect to modern APs.
Upgrading Wi-Fi standards requires updating client and AP hardware.
Wireless network design must manage channel reuse and overlap.
Wi-Fi standards evolve continuously to meet growing demand bandwidth.
